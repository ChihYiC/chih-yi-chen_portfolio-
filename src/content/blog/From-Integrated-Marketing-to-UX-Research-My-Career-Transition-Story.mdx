  My path into UX research did not begin with wireframes, usability testing, or interface design. It began with marketing.

  Before entering the field of UX and Human-Computer Interaction, I worked in integrated marketing, where I gained experience across both online and offline communication. I planned campaigns, created content, supported brand messaging, developed social media strategies, and translated product value into audience-centered stories.

  At first, I saw marketing as a way to communicate value. Over time, I realized that the most effective marketing was not just about promotion. It was about understanding people — what they care about, what they need, what they notice, and what motivates them to take action.

  That realization became the starting point of my transition into UX research.

  ---

  Marketing Taught Me to Think About People


  In marketing, every message has an audience.

  Whether I was working on social media content, product communication, SEO articles, event materials, or brand campaigns, I had to ask similar questions:

  - Who are we speaking to?
  - What problem are they trying to solve?
  - What information matters most to them?
  - What makes this message clear, trustworthy, and engaging?
  - What action do we want them to take next?

  These questions are very similar to the questions UX researchers and designers ask when creating digital experiences.

  Marketing trained me to think beyond the product itself. I learned to consider the audience’s context, expectations, language, attention span, and decision-making process. This helped me understand that communication is not only about what a company wants to say, but also about what people are able to understand, trust, and use.

  ---

  ## Moving from Audience Insight to User Insight

  As I became more interested in UX, I started to see a connection between marketing strategy and user research.

  Marketing often focuses on audience behavior at a broader level: how people respond to messages, campaigns, platforms, and brands. UX research goes deeper into how people interact with systems, interfaces, information, and tasks.

  This shift changed the way I approached problems.

  Instead of only asking, “How can we make this message more attractive?” I began asking:

  - How do users interpret this information?
  - Where do they experience confusion?
  - What prevents them from completing a task?
  - How does the interface shape their trust?
  - What design decision could reduce friction?

  This transition helped me move from communication strategy to experience strategy.

  I began to understand that good design is not only about making something visually appealing. It is about making information easier to understand, decisions easier to make, and interactions more meaningful.

  ---

  ## Why My Marketing Background Makes Me a Stronger UX Researcher

  My marketing background continues to shape how I approach UX research and design.

  First, it helps me understand business goals. In real-world projects, design decisions are rarely made in isolation. They often need to support brand positioning, product adoption, audience engagement, or conversion. My marketing experience allows me to connect user needs with organizational goals.

  Second, it helps me communicate research findings clearly. UX research is not only about collecting data. It is also about translating insights into recommendations that designers, marketers, product teams, and stakeholders can understand and act on.

  Third, it gives me a strong sense of content and messaging. Many user experience problems are not caused by layout alone. They come from unclear wording, weak information hierarchy, hidden explanations, or messages that do not match user expectations.

  Because of this, I often look at UX through both a design lens and a communication lens.

  ---

  ## Connecting UX, HCI, and Human-Centered AI

  My current research interests focus on how people perceive, interpret, and interact with digital content, especially AI-generated media and disclosure labels.

  This interest naturally connects my marketing background with UX and HCI research.

  In digital environments, people are constantly making judgments based on limited information. They decide whether to trust a label, click on a link, believe a piece of content, or engage with a platform. These decisions are shaped by design, wording, visibility, timing, and context.

  For example, my Master’s thesis explored AI disclosure practices in children’s picture books on Amazon. This project examined how AI-related information is disclosed, hidden, or made visible in digital marketplaces. Through this research, I became more interested in transparency, trust, and how interface design affects people’s understanding of AI-generated content.

  To me, this is where UX becomes especially meaningful. UX is not only about usability. It is also about helping people make sense of complex digital systems.

  ---

  ## My Design Perspective Today

  Today, I describe myself as someone who bridges integrated marketing, UX research, and human-centered AI.

  I bring a marketing mindset that values clarity, audience understanding, and strategic communication. I also bring a UX research mindset that values empathy, evidence, usability, and human-centered design.

  Together, these perspectives help me ask better questions:

  - How can digital experiences communicate more clearly?
  - How can design support trust and transparency?
  - How can research reveal what people actually understand?
  - How can business goals and user needs be aligned?
  - How can technology become more human-centered?

  My career transition has taught me that marketing and UX are not separate paths. Both fields are deeply connected by the same core question:

  How do people understand and experience what we create?

  ---

  ## Looking Forward

  Moving from integrated marketing to UX research has allowed me to build a more interdisciplinary perspective.

  I am interested in designing and studying digital experiences that are not only functional, but also clear, trustworthy, and meaningful. Whether I am working on a UX case study, a research project, a content strategy, or a digital product, I care about how people interpret information and how design can support better understanding.

  This is the perspective I continue to bring into my work as a UI/UX designer, HCI researcher, and integrated marketer.
